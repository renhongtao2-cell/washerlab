Overview – What Is the Sun Joe SPX3000, Really?

Let's cut the crap. The Sun Joe SPX3000 is the budget electric pressure washer everybody talks about. It's been the top seller on Amazon for years. You've seen it. Your neighbor probably has one. At roughly , it promises 2030 PSI and 1.76 GPM—numbers that look solid on paper for the price. But I'm not here to read a spec sheet. I'm here to tell you if this thing actually works when you drag it out on a Saturday morning with a dirty driveway staring back at you.

This machine is for the homeowner. The weekend warrior. Someone who needs to wash their car, blast mud off a patio, or clean the mold off the north side of their house maybe once every three months. It is not for a contractor. It is not for scrubbing a fleet of work trucks. It is not for stripping paint off a 40-year-old barn. Know your audience.

I've used the SPX3000 on my own property, on my dad's house, and on a rental I was flipping. I've also run through about eight other electric washers in the last five years. So I know where this thing fits in the lineup.

Key Features – What Stands Out

Sun Joe packs a lot of promises into a box that weighs 24.3 lbs. Here's what caught my eye:

Dual detergent tanks. Two 0.9-liter soap reservoirs built into the frame. You fill one with car soap and the other with house cleaner, and you switch with a dial. Sounds great. In practice… I'll get to that.

14.5-amp motor. That's the standard for this class. It pulls enough juice to make the 2030 PSI believable. Total Stop System (TSS). The motor shuts off when you release the trigger. Saves power, extends pump life. Every electric washer should have this. Not all do.

35-foot power cord. Long enough for most suburban driveways. I rarely needed an extension cord.

20-foot high-pressure hose. Feels short sometimes. I found myself moving the unit more than I wanted to.

Five quick-connect nozzles. 0°, 15°, 25°, 40°, and a soap nozzle. Standard stuff, but the quick-connect works fine. The dual tanks are the headline feature. Nobody else at this price has that. It sounds genius—no swapping bottles. Just turn a knob. But here's the thing: those tanks leak if you overfill them, and the dial gets stiff after a few uses. I'll call that an annoyance, not a dealbreaker.

Performance – How Well Does It Actually Clean?

I spent a Saturday afternoon testing this thing on four surfaces: a dirty 2-car asphalt driveway, a 2010 Toyota Camry, a wooden deck that hadn't been cleaned in two years, and some vinyl siding that was growing green stuff. Here's what happened.

Driveway: I started with the 25° nozzle—my go-to for concrete. The SPX3000 could handle the oil stains from my old Jeep, but it wasn't a one-pass miracle. I had to hold the tip maybe four inches from the surface and move slow. The 1.76 GPM felt anemic compared to my buddy's gas-powered Simpson (2.5 GPM). It took me about 45 minutes to do a driveway I could have finished in 25 with a stronger unit. But the dirt came off. The moss in the cracks? Gone. The driveway looked good—it just took elbow grease.

Car washing: Switched to the 40° nozzle and the foam cannon (sold separately—that's a bit annoying). The soap application was fine. Not the thick foam you see on YouTube detailing channels, but enough to loosen bugs and road grime. Rinsing was solid.

Deck cleaning: This is where the SPX3000 showed its limits. Two years of algae and mildew on a wooden deck is a lot. The 2030 PSI wasn't enough to lift deep-set stains without aggressive back-and-forth motion. I ended up using a brush in addition to the washer. Not ideal, but it got the surface-level grime off.

Vinyl siding: The 25° nozzle worked well here. Green algae came off cleanly without damaging the siding. I kept my distance (about 12 inches) to be safe.

Real-World Use Case

What impressed me: The dual detergent tanks genuinely save time during car washing. Instead of stopping to swap bottles between car soap and driveway cleaner, I could keep both ready. I also appreciated how quiet it was—didn't wake the neighbors at 8 AM on a Saturday.

The most annoying thing: The 20-foot high-pressure hose is genuinely short for the price. I found myself constantly repositioning the unit because I couldn't reach from the garage to the far end of the driveway without moving the whole machine.
